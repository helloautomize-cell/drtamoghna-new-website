H2: Quick *answers*

FAQs:
1. **Do I need an appointment, or can I walk in?**
   Booking is better, because your time is reserved and waiting is shorter. You can book online, call 60031 21297 or WhatsApp 98642 66191, and we confirm within 30 to 60 minutes during clinic hours. If you walk in, we will fit you in when a slot is free, but you may need to wait.
2. **Can I get my echo or ECG done on the same day as the consultation?**
   Usually, yes. Dr. Das first examines you and decides which tests will help.    Bring all your current medicines or their strips, old prescriptions, discharge papers and any test reports from the last year. If you check sugar at home, bring the meter or your diary. A list of your questions helps too. If you are coming for a fasting test, ask us when you book whether to come fasting.
4. **Is Medcare open on Sunday?**
   Yes. Medcare is open Monday to Saturday from 9 am to 8:30 pm and on Sunday from 9 am to 3:30 pm. Hours on festival holidays can change, so please call 60031 21297 before you visit on a holiday.

H1: About *Medcare*

Intro (answer-first): Medcare Diabetes & Obesity Clinic is a physician-led outpatient clinic on Chapaguri Road in North Bongaigaon, Assam. It was founded in September 2023 by Dr. Tamoghna Das, MBBS, MD (Medicine), who sees every patient himself. The clinic looks after diabetes, weight, blood pressure, heart health, thyroid, lung and sleep problems, and does most of the related tests under one roof.

### Why Medcare was started

Long-term conditions rarely come alone. A person with diabetes often has high blood pressure, extra weight or a fatty liver. Over years, these quietly affect the heart, kidneys, eyes, nerves and feet. In many places, patients are sent to different clinics for each problem and lose track of who is looking after what.

Dr. Das started Medcare so that one doctor could look at the full picture. His training in internal medicine covers the whole adult body. His additional training in echocardiography lets him look directly at the heart, which is where diabetes and high blood pressure cause some of their most serious harm.

### How we work

- **You see the doctor, not only an assistant.** Dr. Das takes your history, examines you and explains your plan himself.
- **Tests are chosen, not bundled.** You only do the tests that will change a decision about your care.
- **Reports are explained before you leave.** You go home knowing what each number means and what to do next.
- **We look for complications early.** Eyes, kidneys, nerves, feet and heart are checked on a schedule that suits your condition.
- **Your plan fits your life.** Food advice is based on what you actually eat at home, including rice, fish, pitha and festival food.
- **We keep in touch.** You can send reports and questions on WhatsApp between visits.

### What we do at Medcare

Consultations: diabetes (all types), diabetes in pregnancy, diabetic foot care, obesity and weight, high blood pressure, heart failure and heart risk, thyroid disorders, asthma and COPD, sleep apnea, fever and infections, fatty liver, joint pain and arthritis, migraine and headache, anaemia, and preventive check-ups.

Tests done in the clinic:
- Echocardiography with strain imaging
- ECG
- Patch Holter monitoring (3 days, longer if needed)
- 24-hour blood pressure monitoring
- Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM)
- HbA1c and finger-prick sugar
- Spirometry (lung function)
- Body composition analysis
- Diabetic foot nerve and circulation check
- Sleep study

Medcare does not run its own laboratory or sell medicines at present. Dr. Das tells you which blood tests you need, and you can get them done at a lab of your choice. Prescriptions can be filled at any pharmacy you prefer.

### The clinic

Medcare is on the ground floor, with a wheelchair available. The waiting lounge displays the Charter of Patients' Rights. We accept cash, UPI and cards.

If you need hospital admission, Dr. Das explains your options, and you choose the hospital that suits your needs, location and insurance. Medcare does not favour any particular hospital.

### Intro (answer-first)

Diabetes is a long-term condition in which blood sugar stays higher than normal because the body does not make enough insulin or cannot use it well. At Medcare in North Bongaigaon, Dr. Tamoghna Das looks after adults and young people with type 1, type 2 and other types of diabetes, with the aim of keeping sugar in a safe range and preventing damage to the heart, kidneys, eyes, nerves and feet. HbA1c, CGM, foot checks, ECG and echo are done in the clinic.

### At a glance

- **What it is:** high blood sugar over a long time, often called "sugar" in everyday speech
- **How common:** about 1 in 9 adults in India has diabetes, and many more have prediabetes (ICMR-INDIAB study, 2023)
- **Main tests:** fasting sugar, sugar after food, HbA1c
- **Diagnosis (adults, not pregnant):** fasting sugar 126 mg/dL or more, or HbA1c 6.5% or more, confirmed on repeat testing
- **At Medcare:** consultation, HbA1c, finger-prick sugar, CGM, diabetic foot check, body composition, ECG, echo
- **Goal of care:** steady sugar, fewer highs and lows, and no complications

### Types of diabetes we look after

- **Type 2 diabetes.** The most common type. The body still makes insulin but does not respond to it well. It is linked to family history, extra weight around the waist, low activity, poor sleep and stress. People of South Asian origin often develop it at a younger age and a lower body weight.
- **Type 1 diabetes.** The body makes very little or no insulin, so insulin injections are needed from the start. It usually begins in childhood or early adulthood but can start at any age.
- **Diabetes in pregnancy.** High sugar first found in pregnancy (gestational diabetes) or diabetes that was present before pregnancy. See [Diabetes in pregnancy](/diabetes-care/diabetes-in-pregnancy/).
- **Monogenic and other types.** Rarer forms caused by a single gene change, by pancreas disease, or by medicines such as steroids. These sometimes need a different treatment, so finding the type matters.
- **Prediabetes.** Sugar higher than normal but below the diabetes level (fasting 100 to 125 mg/dL or HbA1c 5.7% to 6.4%). This is the ideal time to act, because changes now can delay or prevent diabetes.

### Signs that can mean high sugar

Many people have no symptoms for years. When symptoms do appear, they can include:
- Passing urine often, especially at night
- Feeling very thirsty
- Tiredness, weakness
- Weight loss without trying
- Blurred vision
- Slow-healing cuts, frequent skin, gum or urine infections
- Tingling or burning in the feet

### Who should get tested

- Adults aged 30 and above, every year or as advised [CONFIRM: Dr. Das's preferred screening age; Indian national programme screens from 30]
- Anyone with a parent, brother or sister with diabetes
- People with a large waist (90 cm or more in men, 80 cm or more in women)
- People with high blood pressure, high cholesterol, fatty liver or PCOS
- Women who had diabetes in a past pregnancy or a baby weighing more than 3.5 kg [CONFIRM: cut-off wording]
- Anyone with the symptoms above

### What happens at Medcare

1. **Detailed history.** Your symptoms, family history, food habits, work hours, sleep, other illnesses and every medicine you take.
2. **Examination.** Weight, waist, BP, pulse, feet, skin and signs of complications.
3. **Tests in the clinic.** HbA1c and finger-prick sugar during the visit. A diabetic foot check for nerve feeling and blood flow. ECG and echo when your heart needs a look, which is common in people who have had diabetes for some years.
4. **Blood and urine tests.** Usually kidney function, urine albumin, cholesterol and others as needed. You can do these at a lab of your choice.
5. **Your plan.** Medicines with clear timings, a food plan built around what you eat at home, an activity goal, and a schedule for sugar checks.
6. **Complication screening.** A yearly plan for eyes, kidneys, feet and heart, so problems are found while they are still small.

Dr. Das decides which tests you need after he has examined you, so you only do the tests that help.

### Treatment, in plain words

Treatment is different for each person. It usually includes:
- **Food:** regular meals, smaller rice portions, more vegetables, dal and protein, fewer sweets and sweet drinks. We work with your usual meals rather than giving a foreign diet chart.
- **Activity:** most adults benefit from about 150 minutes of brisk walking or similar activity a week, spread over most days, plus some muscle-strengthening work.
- **Medicines:** tablets, injections or insulin, chosen for your type of diabetes, your kidney and heart health, your weight and your budget.
- **Monitoring:** home sugar checks or CGM, and HbA1c every 3 months until stable.
- **Protection:** BP and cholesterol control, foot care, and vaccines as advised.

Glass cards: Diabetes in pregnancy · Diabetic foot care · CGM, insulin and diet counselling · Obesity and weight management · GLP-1 weight-loss medicines

### How to prepare for your visit

- Bring your medicines (or strips), your sugar meter or diary and reports from the last year
- If a fasting test may be needed, ask us before the visit how to manage your morning dose
- Write down any low sugar episodes: time, reading and what you were doing

### After your visit

You leave with written medicine timings, sugar targets, food and activity advice, and a follow-up date. Most people are reviewed every 1 to 3 months until sugar is steady, then every 3 to 6 months. You can WhatsApp sugar readings and reports between visits.

### When to see a doctor urgently

Callout:
> Call 108 or 112, or go to a hospital emergency, if a person with diabetes has: confusion, fainting or a seizure; sugar below 70 mg/dL that does not come up after taking sugar; sugar above 300 mg/dL with vomiting, deep fast breathing or drowsiness; chest pain or breathlessness; or a foot that is suddenly red, hot, swollen or turning black.

### FAQs

1. **Can diabetes be cured?**
   Type 1 diabetes cannot be cured at present. Type 2 diabetes is long-term, but with weight loss, food changes and activity, some people bring their sugar into the normal range and need fewer medicines. This needs regular checks, because sugar can rise again. Be careful of anyone promising a permanent cure or "insulin-free" treatment.
2. **What is a good HbA1c?**
   For many adults with diabetes the target is below 7%, but it depends on age, other illnesses and the risk of low sugar. An older person who lives alone may have a slightly higher target to stay safe. Dr. Das will set your personal target and explain it.
3. **Once I start insulin, do I have to take it for life?**
   Not always. People with type 1 diabetes need insulin for life. In type 2 diabetes, insulin is sometimes started to bring very high sugar down and later reduced or stopped when other treatment works. Insulin is not a sign of failure. It is one of the safest and most effective ways to control sugar.
4. **Can I eat rice if I have diabetes?**
   Yes, in sensible amounts. Fill half your plate with vegetables, a quarter with dal, fish, egg, paneer or meat, and a quarter with rice or roti. Eating vegetables and protein first and walking after meals also helps. Dr. Das will help you plan meals around foods you enjoy.
5. **Why does a diabetes doctor check my heart?**
   Diabetes raises the risk of heart attack and heart failure, sometimes without the usual warning symptoms. An ECG and echo, including strain imaging, can show early changes in heart function. Finding these early means BP, cholesterol and sugar can be treated more firmly to protect the heart.
6. **How often should I check my sugar at home?**
   It depends on your treatment. People on insulin often check several times a day or use CGM. People on tablets alone may check a few times a week, at different times. Dr. Das will tell you when to check and what to do with the numbers.
7. **Is diabetes in young people the same as in older adults?**
   Not always. Young people can have type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes or a rarer monogenic type. The right treatment depends on the type, so Dr. Das looks carefully at age, weight, family history and test results before deciding.

### Intro

Diabetes in pregnancy means high blood sugar during pregnancy, either first found while pregnant (gestational diabetes, or GDM) or present from before. At Medcare, Dr. Tamoghna Das helps pregnant women in and around Bongaigaon keep sugar in a safe range, working alongside your obstetrician, so that you and your baby stay healthy.

### At a glance

- **Who is tested in India:** every pregnant woman, at the first antenatal visit and again at 24 to 28 weeks (national guidelines)
- **The test:** 75 g glucose drink, with a blood sugar check after 2 hours; 140 mg/dL or more means GDM. You do not need to come fasting for this test.
- **Why it matters:** high sugar can make the baby grow too large, raise the risk of a difficult delivery, and cause low sugar in the newborn
- **At Medcare:** consultation, finger-prick sugar, HbA1c, CGM, diet counselling, insulin counselling
- **Your obstetrician** stays in charge of your pregnancy. Medcare looks after your sugar and shares notes with them.

### Who is at higher risk

- Family history of diabetes
- Being overweight before pregnancy
- Age above 25 to 30 years [CONFIRM: wording]
- GDM in a past pregnancy, or a past baby weighing more than 3.5 kg
- PCOS
- South Asian background, which by itself raises the risk

Many women with GDM have no symptoms, which is why testing every pregnant woman matters.

### What happens at Medcare

1. **First visit.** Dr. Das reviews your antenatal card, scans and sugar results, checks BP and weight, and asks about your meals and daily routine.
2. **Sugar targets.** You get clear targets for fasting and after-meal sugar, usually checked at home with a meter. CGM can be used when sugar is hard to read or changes a lot.
3. **Food plan.** Small regular meals, the right amount of rice or roti at each meal, enough protein, and safe snacks. The aim is steady sugar without leaving you or your baby short of nutrition.
4. **Medicine when needed.** If food changes alone do not keep sugar on target, Dr. Das will discuss medicine. Insulin is safe in pregnancy and is widely used. He explains every step, including how to inject and how to avoid low sugar.
5. **Thyroid and BP checks.** Thyroid problems and high BP are also common in pregnancy and are checked as needed.
6. **Shared care.** Your readings and plan are shared with your obstetrician on your request.

### How to prepare

- Bring your antenatal card, all scan and blood reports, and your sugar diary if you have one
- Bring the medicines and supplements you are taking
- For the 75 g glucose test, you can come at any time of day; it does not need fasting

### After delivery

GDM usually goes away after the baby is born, but it is a warning sign for the future. Women who had GDM have a much higher chance of developing type 2 diabetes later in life.
- A sugar test is advised about 6 to 12 weeks after delivery
- Then a sugar test every year, or as advised
- Breastfeeding, healthy weight and activity lower the risk
- Before the next pregnancy, have your sugar checked early

### When to see a doctor urgently

> Go to your maternity hospital or call 108 or 112 if you have: severe headache with blurred vision, swelling of face or hands, reduced baby movements, bleeding or leaking fluid, sugar below 70 mg/dL that does not come up after taking sugar, or vomiting that stops you keeping food down.

### FAQs

1. **Will gestational diabetes harm my baby?**
   When sugar is well controlled, most women with GDM have healthy babies. High sugar that is not treated can make the baby grow large, make delivery harder and cause low sugar in the baby after birth. Regular checks, a good food plan and medicine if needed reduce these risks.
2. **Is insulin safe during pregnancy?**
   Yes. Insulin does not cross to the baby in a way that harms it, and it has been used safely in pregnancy for many decades. If your sugar stays high despite food changes, insulin is a common and safe choice. Dr. Das will teach you how to use it and how to spot low sugar.
3. **Do I need to come fasting for the GDM test?**
   No. In India, the national test for pregnant women uses a 75 g glucose drink given at any time of day, regardless of when you last ate. Blood sugar is checked 2 hours later. A result of 140 mg/dL or more means gestational diabetes.
4. **Will I still have diabetes after the baby is born?**
   In most women, sugar returns to normal after delivery. However, GDM raises the chance of type 2 diabetes later, so a sugar test is advised about 6 to 12 weeks after delivery and then every year. Staying active and keeping a healthy weight lower this risk.
5. **Can I fast during pregnancy if I have GDM?**
   Long fasts are not advised, because they can cause low sugar followed by high sugar. If fasting matters to you for religious or festival reasons, talk to Dr. Das beforehand so a safe plan can be made with your obstetrician.
6. **I had diabetes before pregnancy. What should I do?**
   Ideally, see a doctor before you become pregnant to bring HbA1c into a safe range and check your medicines, because some tablets are not suitable in pregnancy. If you are already pregnant, book an early visit so treatment can be adjusted quickly.

### Intro

Diabetes can slowly damage the nerves and blood vessels of the feet, so a small cut or blister may go unnoticed and turn into a serious wound. At Medcare in Bongaigaon, Dr. Tamoghna Das, who has trained in diabetic foot management, checks feet for early nerve and blood-flow problems and helps you protect them. Most foot problems in diabetes can be prevented.

### At a glance

- **Who needs a check:** everyone with diabetes, at least once a year, and at every visit if your feet are at risk
- **What we check:** feeling (nerves), pulses and blood flow, skin, nails, foot shape and footwear
- **Tests at Medcare:** monofilament and vibration testing for nerves, Doppler for blood flow [CONFIRM: tests used, generic names only]
- **Common causes of wounds:** walking barefoot, tight shoes, hot water bottles, cutting corns at home
- **Key message:** look at your feet every day

### Why feet are at risk in diabetes

- **Nerve damage (neuropathy):** feet lose feeling, so injuries are not felt. It can also cause burning, tingling or pins and needles, often worse at night.
- **Poor blood flow:** narrowed arteries mean wounds heal slowly.
- **Dry, cracked skin:** cracks let infection in.
- **Changes in foot shape:** pressure points form, leading to corns and calluses that can break down.

### Warning signs

- Numbness, tingling or burning in the feet
- Cracks in the heels, corns or hard skin
- A blister, cut or sore that is slow to heal
- Change of colour: red, blue or very pale skin
- Swelling, warmth, or a change in foot shape
- Pain in the calves on walking that settles with rest
- Thick or ingrown nails, or a fungal infection between the toes

### What happens at Medcare

1. **Foot history.** Past wounds, pain, numbness, footwear and daily habits.
2. **Examination.** Skin, nails, pressure areas, pulses and foot shape.
3. **Nerve check.** Simple painless tests of feeling and vibration.
4. **Blood-flow check.** Pulses and a handheld Doppler test of the foot arteries.
5. **Risk grade.** You are told whether your feet are low, moderate or high risk, and how often they need checking.
6. **Plan.** Daily foot care, footwear advice, sugar and BP control, and treatment for burning pain. For wounds, Dr. Das advises on dressing and infection, and explains if you need a surgeon or a hospital.

### Daily foot care at home

- Look at both feet every day, including between the toes and under the heel. Use a mirror or ask someone to help.
- Wash in lukewarm water, test the temperature with your elbow, and dry well between the toes.
- Use moisturiser on dry skin, but not between the toes.
- Cut nails straight across. Do not cut corns or use corn plasters.
- Never walk barefoot, at home, in the yard, at prayer places or in the paddy field.
- Wear soft, well-fitting closed footwear with cotton socks. Check inside shoes before wearing them.
- Do not use hot water bottles or heat pads on numb feet.
- Stop smoking, which reduces blood flow.

### When to see a doctor urgently

> Come the same day, or go to a hospital emergency if the clinic is closed, if you have: a foot wound with redness spreading, swelling, pus or a bad smell; fever with a foot wound; skin turning black or blue; or a sudden hot, red, swollen foot even without a wound. Call 108 or 112 if you are drowsy, confused or very unwell.

### FAQs

1. **How often should my feet be checked?**
   Everyone with diabetes should have a full foot check at least once a year. If you have lost feeling, have poor blood flow, or have had a wound before, your feet need checking at every visit, often every 1 to 3 months. Daily self-checks at home are just as important.
2. **My feet burn at night. What can help?**
   Burning, tingling and pins and needles are common signs of diabetic nerve damage. Better sugar control, vitamin B12 checks and specific medicines for nerve pain can help. Dr. Das will examine your feet, check for other causes and suggest treatment.
3. **Can I use home remedies on a diabetic foot wound?**
   Please do not apply home remedies, powders, oils or heat to a wound. They can hide infection or burn numb skin. Keep the wound clean and covered, take weight off the foot and get it seen quickly. Early care is the surest way to avoid serious problems.
4. **What kind of footwear is best?**
   Closed, soft, roomy shoes with a firm sole and no rough seams inside. Avoid tight shoes, high heels and thin rubber slippers. People with high-risk feet may need special footwear or insoles. Buy shoes in the evening, when feet are slightly swollen.
5. **Can foot nerve damage be reversed?**
   Nerve damage usually cannot be fully undone, but good sugar control can slow it and sometimes ease symptoms. The bigger aim is protection: daily checks, proper footwear and early treatment of small problems prevent most serious wounds.

### Intro

Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) uses a small sensor on the upper arm to record your sugar every few minutes, day and night, for up to two weeks. A finger-prick tells you your sugar at one moment. CGM shows the whole day:

5. **What should I do if my sugar goes low?**

   No. Most people in Assam eat rice daily, and a good plan can include it. The amount, what you eat with it, and what you do after the meal matter more. CGM can show you how much rice your body handles well.

- Cut sweet drinks, packaged snacks and fried snacks first. These are the easiest wins.
- Keep sweets and pitha for festivals, in small portions.
- Walk daily. Start with 10 minutes after meals and build up.
- Add simple strength work twice a week to protect muscle.
- Aim for 7 hours of sleep.

- **Treatment:** less salt, activity, weight, sleep, limiting alcohol and tobacco, and medicines when needed

### Getting the diagnosis right

One high reading is not enough to diagnose high BP. Readings rise with stress, rushing, pain or a full bladder, and some people's BP is higher only in the clinic ("white-coat" effect). Others have normal clinic readings but high BP at home ("masked" high BP).

At Medcare, BP is confirmed with:
- Repeated readings after rest
- Home BP readings taken the right way
- **24-hour BP monitoring:** a small cuff and recorder that measure BP every 15 to 30 minutes through a normal day and night. This shows your real average, your night-time BP and how your medicines work across the day. Night readings are a strong sign of future heart risk.

1. **History and examination.** Symptoms, family history, salt intake, sleep, snoring, painkiller use and other medicines.
2. **Confirm the diagnosis.** Clinic and home readings, and 24-hour monitoring when needed.
3. **Check for damage.** ECG and echo to see whether the heart muscle has thickened or the heart is under strain. Kidney and urine tests. Eye checks as advised.
4. **Look for causes.** Especially in young people or when BP is very hard to control: kidney problems, hormone conditions, sleep apnea and some medicines.
5. **Treatment plan.** Lifestyle steps and, when needed, medicines chosen for your age, kidneys, heart and other conditions.
6. **Follow-up.** Home BP targets, review visits and medicine adjustment until BP is steady.

### Checking BP at home the right way

- Sit quietly for 5 minutes with your back supported and feet on the floor
- Rest your arm on a table at heart level, with the cuff on a bare arm
- No tea, coffee, smoking or exercise for 30 minutes before
- Take 2 readings, 1 minute apart, morning and evening, for 7 days before your visit
- Write down every reading, or bring the machine with its memory

### Lowering BP day to day

- Keep salt under about one level teaspoon a day in total, including salt already in food
- Limit pickles, papad, dried fish, chips, instant noodles and salty snacks
- Eat more vegetables, fruit, dal and curd
- Walk briskly 30 minutes on most days
- Lose weight if needed, especially around the waist
- Limit alcohol and stop all tobacco
- Get loud snoring checked: sleep apnea is a common hidden cause of high BP

### When to see a doctor urgently

> Call 108 or 112 if high BP comes with chest pain, severe breathlessness, sudden severe headache, confusion, blurred vision, weakness on one side of the body, difficulty speaking, or fits. A very high reading (180/120 mmHg or more) with any of these is an emergency.

### FAQs

1. **I feel fine. Why should I take BP medicine?**
   High BP usually has no symptoms until it causes a heart attack, stroke or kidney damage. Feeling well does not mean your arteries are safe. Treatment lowers these risks a great deal. Many people need medicines long term, and some can reduce them with weight loss and less salt.
2. **Can I stop BP tablets once my BP is normal?**
   Please do not stop on your own. Normal readings usually mean the medicine is working. Stopping suddenly can make BP rise again, sometimes sharply. If your BP stays low or you make big lifestyle changes, Dr. Das may reduce the dose carefully under follow-up.
3. **What is 24-hour BP monitoring?**
   It is a small cuff on your arm connected to a recorder at your waist. It measures BP every 15 to 30 minutes for a full day and night while you go about normal life. It shows your true average BP, night-time BP and whether readings in the clinic are misleading.
4. **Is my BP machine at home accurate?**
   Most automatic arm machines are fine if used correctly. Wrist machines are less reliable. Bring your machine to the clinic once so readings can be compared. Use the right cuff size, and sit and rest properly before checking.
5. **Can high BP affect my heart even if I have no chest pain?**
   Yes. Long-standing high BP makes the heart muscle thicker and stiffer, which can lead to heart failure over time. An echo shows these changes early. Good BP control can stop them getting worse.
6. **Does salt really matter that much?**

At Medcare in Bongaigaon, Dr. Tamoghna Das diagnoses heart failure with echocardiography and provides ongoing care to ease symptoms, keep you active and reduce hospital stays.

### At a glance

- **Common symptoms:** breathlessness on walking or lying flat, swollen feet, tiredness, waking up breathless at night
- **Common causes:** high BP, diabetes, coronary artery disease, valve disease, some infections and alcohol
- **Main test:** echocardiography (heart ultrasound), with strain imaging to detect early changes
- **Earlier stages:** heart changes can be found on echo before symptoms begin ("stage B" heart failure)
- **At Medcare:** echo, ECG, Holter, BP and sugar control, medicine plan and regular follow-up
- **Daily habit that helps most:** weigh yourself every morning

### Stages of heart failure, in plain words

- **Stage A, at risk:** high BP, diabetes, obesity or other risk factors, but a normal heart
- **Stage B, early heart changes:** the echo shows changes, such as a stiff or thickened heart or reduced strain, but no symptoms yet
- **Stage C, symptoms:** breathlessness, swelling or tiredness due to heart changes
- **Stage D, advanced:** symptoms at rest despite treatment, needing specialist care

1. **History and examination.** Symptoms, how far you can walk, sleep position, swelling, weight change and past illnesses.
2. **Echo and ECG.** To see how well the heart pumps and fills, the valves, pressures and rhythm. Strain imaging adds detail on heart muscle function.
3. **Blood tests.** Kidney function, salts, haemoglobin, thyroid, sugar and, when needed, a heart failure marker (NT-proBNP), done at a lab of your choice.
4. **Treatment plan.** Medicines that ease fluid build-up and medicines proven to help the heart work better and live longer, started and adjusted step by step.
5. **Self-care training.** Daily weight, fluid and salt guidance, activity, vaccines, and warning signs.
6. **Follow-up.** Regular reviews to adjust medicines, check kidneys and salts, and repeat echo when useful. If you need a device or procedure, Dr. Das will explain your options.

### Living well with heart failure

- Weigh yourself every morning after passing urine and before breakfast. Write it down.
- A gain of 2 kg in 3 days is a warning sign: call the clinic.
- Keep salt low and follow the fluid advice you are given.
- Take every medicine daily. Do not stop because you feel better.
- Stay active with walking at a comfortable pace.
- Avoid painkillers such as common joint pain tablets unless your doctor agrees; some worsen heart failure.
- Get flu and pneumonia vaccines as advised.

### When to see a doctor urgently

> Call 108 or 112 if you have severe breathlessness, breathlessness at rest or when lying flat that is getting worse, pink frothy cough, chest pain, fainting, or confusion. Call the clinic the same day if your weight rises by 2 kg in 3 days or swelling increases.

### FAQs

1. **Does heart failure mean my heart is about to stop?**
   No. Heart failure means the heart is weaker or stiffer than normal and cannot meet the body's needs fully. Many people live active lives for years with good treatment. The right medicines, salt and fluid control and regular follow-up make a big difference to symptoms and outlook.
2. **Can heart failure get better?**
   In some people, heart function improves a lot with treatment, especially when the cause, such as high BP or a fast heart rhythm, is controlled. In others the aim is to keep it stable and prevent worsening. Regular echo checks show how the heart is responding.
3. **What is stage B heart failure?**
   Stage B means the echo shows changes in the heart's structure or function, but there are no symptoms yet. It is common in people with diabetes and high BP. Finding it allows firmer control of BP, sugar and other factors to try to stop progression to symptoms.
4. **Why should I weigh myself every day?**
   Fluid can build up in the body a few days before breathlessness gets worse. A sudden weight gain, such as 2 kg in 3 days, is often the earliest sign. Catching it early means your medicines can be adjusted before you need hospital care.
5. **Can I exercise with heart failure?**
   Yes, in most cases, and it helps. Regular walking at a pace where you can still talk improves strength and breathing. Dr. Das will tell you what is safe for you and when to rest.
6. **Do I need to drink less water?**
   Some people with heart failure need to limit fluids, but not everyone. It depends on your symptoms, kidney function and salt levels. Follow the specific fluid advice Dr. Das gives you rather than general rules.

An echocardiogram, or echo, is an ultrasound scan of the heart. It shows how well the heart pumps and relaxes, the size of its chambers, how the valves work and the pressures inside. ### What echo can show

- Pumping strength of the heart (ejection fraction)
- How well the heart relaxes and fills (diastolic function), often affected by high BP and diabetes

- Valve narrowing or leakage
- Holes in the heart and other structural problems
- Fluid around the heart
- Raised pressure in the lungs
- Areas of muscle that move poorly after a heart attack

### What strain imaging adds

A standard echo measures how much blood the heart pumps out. Strain imaging, also called speckle tracking, follows tiny patterns in the heart muscle to measure how well each part of the muscle shortens and stretches.

- **LV strain (global longitudinal strain):** can fall before the ejection fraction drops. Useful in diabetes, high BP, some cancer treatments and suspected early heart failure.
- **LA strain:** shows how the left upper chamber is coping, linked to filling pressure and rhythm problems.
- **RV strain:** helps assess the right side of the heart, for example in lung disease or raised lung pressure.

This lets Dr. Das spot subtle heart muscle problems early and treat the causes more firmly.

### Who may need an echo

- Breathlessness, chest discomfort, swelling, palpitations or fainting
- A heart murmur heard by a doctor
- Long-standing diabetes or high BP
- Abnormal ECG

- Family history of certain heart muscle diseases

### FAQs

3. **Is an echo safe?**
   Yes. Echo uses sound waves, not X-rays or radiation. It is painless and safe for all ages, including in pregnancy. You may feel slight pressure from the probe.
4. **What is a normal ejection fraction?**
   Ejection fraction is the share of blood the main pumping chamber pushes out with each beat. A value of about 55% or more is usually normal. A normal value does not always mean a normal heart, which is why relaxation and strain are also checked.
5. **Why does strain imaging matter if my ejection fraction is normal?**
   The ejection fraction can stay normal while the heart muscle is already under stress, for example in diabetes or high BP. Strain imaging can pick up these early changes, giving a chance to treat the cause before the pumping strength drops.
6. **Can an echo detect blocked arteries?**
   Not directly. An echo shows how the heart muscle moves, and it may show areas that are not moving well after a heart attack. Blocked arteries are usually assessed with other tests, such as a treadmill test, CT angiography or angiography. Dr. Das will advise if you need one.

   Yes. Some strokes are caused by an irregular rhythm called atrial fibrillation, which can come and go. Longer recording can find it, and finding it changes treatment to prevent another stroke.

### Intro

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a condition in which the throat repeatedly narrows or closes during sleep, so breathing stops for a few seconds many times a night. It causes loud snoring, poor sleep and daytime sleepiness, and it raises the risk of high blood pressure, heart rhythm problems, diabetes and road accidents. At Medcare in Bongaigaon, Dr. Tamoghna Das, who holds a Diploma in Obstructive Sleep Apnea, tests for sleep apnea and other sleep problems and plans treatment.

### At a glance

- **Common signs:** loud snoring, pauses in breathing seen by a partner, choking or gasping at night, daytime sleepiness, morning headache
- **Higher risk:** extra weight, a thick neck, high BP that is hard to control, diabetes, older age, men, and women after menopause
- **Main test:** a sleep study that records breathing and oxygen overnight
- **Main treatments:** weight loss, sleep position, a CPAP machine that keeps the airway open, and treatment of nose or throat problems
- **At Medcare:** consultation, sleep study, report reading, treatment plan and follow-up

### Why sleep apnea matters

Every pause in breathing lowers oxygen and jolts the body awake for a moment. This happens dozens or even hundreds of times a night without you knowing. Over time it:
- Raises blood pressure, often in a way that resists medicines
- Increases the risk of atrial fibrillation, heart failure and stroke
- Makes sugar harder to control
- Causes tiredness, poor concentration and low mood
- Raises the chance of accidents while driving or working with machines

### Other sleep problems we look at

- Difficulty falling or staying asleep (insomnia)
- Restless legs at night
- Shift-work sleep problems
- Sleepiness due to medicines, thyroid problems or anaemia

### What happens at Medcare

1. **Sleep history.** Your snoring, sleep times, daytime sleepiness, and what your partner has noticed. A short questionnaire helps score your risk.
2. **Examination.** Weight, neck size, BP, nose and throat.
3. **Sleep study.** Sensors record breathing, oxygen level, pulse and body position through one night. [CONFIRM: home kit or overnight at the clinic]
4. **Results.** Dr. Das explains how many breathing pauses you have per hour and how severe the sleep apnea is.
5. **Treatment plan.** Depending on severity: weight loss, avoiding sleeping on your back, avoiding alcohol and sleeping pills at night, treatment of a blocked nose, CPAP, or referral for a dental device or surgery opinion.
6. **Follow-up.** Checking that treatment improves sleep, BP and daytime energy.

### FAQs

1. **Is loud snoring always sleep apnea?**
   No. Many people snore without sleep apnea. But loud snoring with pauses in breathing, gasping, or feeling sleepy during the day suggests sleep apnea and should be checked. A sleep study gives the answer.
2. **What is a CPAP machine?**
   CPAP stands for continuous positive airway pressure. A small machine blows gentle air through a mask while you sleep, keeping the throat open. It is the most effective treatment for moderate and severe sleep apnea. Most people sleep better and feel more alert within weeks.
3. **Can losing weight get rid of sleep apnea?**
   Losing weight often reduces sleep apnea, and in some people it improves a lot. It does not always remove it completely, especially when the airway is naturally narrow. A repeat sleep study after significant weight loss shows whether treatment can be reduced.
4. **Can sleep apnea cause high BP?**
   Yes. Repeated drops in oxygen at night raise BP, and sleep apnea is one of the most common causes of BP that stays high despite several medicines. Treating sleep apnea can help lower BP.
5. **Should I take sleeping pills for poor sleep?**
   Not without a doctor's advice. Sleeping pills and alcohol relax the throat muscles and can make sleep apnea worse. If you sleep poorly, Dr. Das will look for the cause first.
6. **Is it safe to drive if I have sleep apnea?**
   If you feel sleepy during the day, avoid driving until you are treated. Untreated sleep apnea raises the risk of road accidents. With effective treatment, most people can drive safely again.

3. **What is the difference between asthma and COPD?**

4. **Can COPD be reversed?**
   Lung damage in COPD does not fully reverse, but treatment can greatly improve breathing, activity and quality of life. Stopping smoking slows further damage at any age. Inhalers, vaccines, exercise and quick treatment of flare-ups help most people stay active.

### Intro

Fatty liver means extra fat has built up inside the liver. It is very common, often found by chance on an ultrasound, and is closely linked to extra weight, diabetes, high cholesterol and alcohol. ### At a glance

- **New name:** fatty liver linked to weight, sugar or cholesterol is now called MASLD (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease), earlier called NAFLD
- **How common:** studies suggest about 1 in 3 adults in India has fatty liver
- **Main causes:** extra weight around the waist, type 2 diabetes, high triglycerides, alcohol, some medicines, rapid weight loss, hepatitis B or C
- **What matters most:** not the fat itself but whether there is scarring (fibrosis)
- **At Medcare:** consultation, body composition, sugar and HbA1c, review of ultrasound and blood tests, fibrosis risk scoring, and a plan
- **Main treatment:** gradual weight loss, better sugar control, less alcohol, activity

### Grades on ultrasound, explained

An ultrasound report may say grade 1, 2 or 3 fatty liver. This shows how much fat is seen, but it does not show scarring, which is what predicts future liver problems. A grade 1 report can still hide scarring, and a grade 3 report may not have any. That is why further assessment matters.

### What happens at Medcare

1. **History.** Weight change, alcohol, medicines, family history, diabetes, and symptoms such as tiredness or upper right abdominal discomfort.
2. **Examination.** Weight, waist, body composition, BP, and signs of liver disease.
3. **Tests.** Liver function, blood count, sugar, cholesterol and hepatitis tests at a lab of your choice. A simple score called FIB-4, calculated from routine blood tests, estimates scarring risk.
4. **Further checks when needed.** If the score is not low, Dr. Das may advise a liver stiffness scan (elastography) at a centre that offers it, or a specialist opinion.
5. **Plan.** Weight, food, activity, alcohol advice, and control of diabetes, cholesterol and BP. Heart risk is also assessed, because people with fatty liver more often die of heart disease than of liver disease.
6. **Follow-up.** Liver tests and scores rechecked to see progress.

### Protecting your liver

- Lose weight gradually: even 5% helps, and 7% to 10% can reduce inflammation and scarring
- Cut sugary drinks, sweets and refined flour; reduce rice portions
- Eat more vegetables, dal, fish and whole grains
- Walk briskly 30 minutes on most days and add strength exercise
- Avoid alcohol, or keep it to a minimum as advised
- Do not take herbal or "liver tonic" products without advice; some can harm the liver
- Get vaccinated against hepatitis B if not already protected

### When to see a doctor urgently

> Seek urgent care if you notice yellow eyes or skin, vomiting blood, black stools, a swelling belly, confusion or excessive drowsiness, or severe pain in the upper right abdomen with fever.

### FAQs

1. **Is fatty liver serious?**
   For most people, fatty liver is a warning sign rather than an immediate danger. In some it causes inflammation and scarring, which can progress over years to cirrhosis. It also raises the risk of diabetes and heart disease. Checking for scarring and treating the cause is what keeps it from becoming serious.
2. **Can fatty liver go away?**
   Yes, in many people the fat reduces and liver tests improve with gradual weight loss, better sugar control and avoiding alcohol. Losing about 7% to 10% of body weight can reduce inflammation and even some scarring. Regular follow-up shows how the liver is responding.
3. **I am thin. Can I still have fatty liver?**
   Yes. Many Indians have fatty liver at a normal body weight, often because of fat around the waist, low muscle, diabetes or high triglycerides. This is sometimes called lean fatty liver and needs the same careful assessment.
4. **Are there medicines for fatty liver?**
   Lifestyle change is the main treatment. Some medicines used for diabetes or weight can also help the liver, and newer treatments exist for selected people with scarring. Dr. Das will advise if a medicine suits you. Be careful with unproven liver tonics and supplements.
5. **Should I stop eating oil completely?**
   No. You need some fat in your diet. Reduce deep-fried food and use measured amounts of cooking oil. Cutting sugar, sweet drinks and large portions of rice usually helps the liver more than cutting oil alone.

4. **Find the cause.** For example, heavy periods, worms or a bleeding source. Older adults with iron deficiency may need a stomach and bowel check, and Dr. Das will explain why.

6. **Follow-up.** Haemoglobin rechecked after a few weeks to make sure treatment is working.

### Food that helps

- Iron-rich foods: green leafy vegetables, dal, chana, rajma, eggs, fish, meat, liver, jaggery in moderation
- Vitamin C with meals (lemon, amla, guava, orange) helps iron absorption
- Avoid tea or coffee within an hour of iron-rich meals or iron tablets
- Vitamin B12 comes mainly from milk, curd, eggs, fish and meat; strict vegetarians may need supplements

### When to see a doctor urgently

> Seek urgent care or call 108 or 112 for: breathlessness at rest, chest pain, fainting, black or bloody stools, vomiting blood, or very heavy bleeding.

### FAQs

1. **Should I just take iron tablets for anaemia?**
   Not without knowing the cause. Iron helps only if you are short of iron, and anaemia can also come from low B12, inherited haemoglobin conditions, kidney disease or long-term illness. Taking iron when it is not needed can cause harm. A few simple tests show the cause.
2. **Why is anaemia so common in women?**
   Monthly periods, pregnancy, breastfeeding and diets low in iron all reduce iron stores. More than half of Indian women of reproductive age have anaemia. Treating heavy periods, eating iron-rich food and taking supplements when advised make a real difference.
3. **What is haemoglobin E trait?**
   It is an inherited change in haemoglobin that is common in Northeast India. Carriers usually have no or mild symptoms but may have small red cells that look like iron deficiency on reports. Knowing about it avoids unnecessary iron and matters for family planning.
4. **Can diabetes medicines cause anaemia?**
   Long-term use of a common diabetes medicine can lower vitamin B12 in some people, which can cause anaemia and nerve problems. Kidney disease from diabetes can also cause anaemia. People with long-standing diabetes should have B12 and haemoglobin checked from time to time.
5. **How long does it take to recover?**
   With the right treatment, haemoglobin usually starts rising within 2 to 4 weeks, and iron stores take about 3 months to fill. Continue treatment as advised even after you feel better. A follow-up blood test confirms recovery.

H1: HbA1c explained: what your 3-month sugar *number* means

**In brief**
- HbA1c shows your average blood sugar over roughly the last 3 months.
- Below 5.7% is normal, 5.7% to 6.4% is prediabetes, and 6.5% or more suggests diabetes.
- Many adults with diabetes aim for below 7%, but your own target may differ.
- You do not need to fast for an HbA1c test.
- Anaemia, some haemoglobin conditions and recent blood loss can make HbA1c misleading.

### What HbA1c measures

Sugar in the blood sticks to haemoglobin, the protein inside red blood cells. The higher your sugar has been, the more haemoglobin gets "sugar-coated". Red blood cells live for about 3 months, so the share of coated haemoglobin, shown as a percentage, reflects your average sugar over that time. Recent weeks count more than older ones.

That is why HbA1c is so useful. A single fasting sugar shows one moment. HbA1c shows the bigger picture.

### What the numbers mean

| HbA1c | What it suggests |
|---|---|
| Below 5.7% | Normal |
| 5.7% to 6.4% | Prediabetes: higher risk of diabetes; a good time to act |
| 6.5% or more | Diabetes, usually confirmed with a repeat test |

For people who already have diabetes, HbA1c shows how well the current plan is working.

### What should my target be?

For many adults with diabetes, the target is below 7%. Some people, such as younger adults early in diabetes, may aim lower if they can do it without low sugar. Others, such as older adults living alone, people with frequent lows or serious heart or kidney disease, may have a slightly higher target to stay safe.

Your target is a decision between you and your doctor. At Medcare, Dr. Das explains your number and your target at every review.

### How often should it be checked?

- Every 3 months when treatment is being changed or sugar is not on target
- Every 6 months when sugar is steady and on target
- Once a year as part of a check-up for adults at risk of diabetes

### When HbA1c can mislead

HbA1c depends on red blood cells, so anything that changes them can make the number look higher or lower than your real sugar:
- Iron deficiency anaemia can push HbA1c up
- Recent blood loss, blood transfusion or pregnancy can push it down
- Inherited haemoglobin conditions, such as haemoglobin E trait, common in Northeast India, can affect some test methods
- Advanced kidney disease can make results less reliable

If your HbA1c does not match your home readings, Dr. Das may suggest continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) to see what is really happening.

> **Tip:** you do not need to fast for HbA1c. At Medcare, HbA1c can be tested during your visit, with the result ready before you leave.

### Bringing HbA1c down

Small steady changes add up over 3 months:
- Smaller rice portions, with more vegetables and protein on the plate
- A 10 to 15 minute walk after main meals
- Fewer sweet drinks and sweets
- Regular sleep
- Taking medicines on time, every day
- Checking sugar at home as advised

### FAQs

1. **Do I need to fast for HbA1c?**
   No. HbA1c reflects your average sugar over about 3 months, so a recent meal does not change it. It can be checked at any time of day. If other fasting tests are planned on the same day, follow the fasting advice for those.
2. **Can HbA1c be normal even if my sugar is high after meals?**
   Yes, sometimes. HbA1c is an average, so high readings after meals can be balanced by lower readings at other times. Early in diabetes, after-meal sugar often rises first. CGM or after-meal checks help find this pattern.
3. **How quickly can HbA1c change?**
   HbA1c changes slowly, because it reflects about 3 months. You may see some change after 6 to 8 weeks, but the full effect of a new plan shows at about 3 months. That is why it is usually rechecked every 3 months.
4. **Is HbA1c alone enough to diagnose diabetes?**
   An HbA1c of 6.5% or more suggests diabetes, and it is usually confirmed with a repeat test or a sugar test. If you have clear symptoms and a high random sugar, one test may be enough. Your doctor will interpret it alongside your history.

Sources: American Diabetes Association, Standards of Care in Diabetes (current edition) · ICMR guidelines for management of type 2 diabetes

H1: Why people with diabetes or high BP may need an *echo*

**In brief**
- Diabetes and high blood pressure can change the heart muscle years before any symptoms.
- An echo is a painless ultrasound that shows how the heart pumps and relaxes.
- Strain imaging can pick up early heart muscle changes even when the standard echo looks normal.
- Finding changes early allows firmer control of BP, sugar and cholesterol to protect the heart.
- Not everyone needs an echo; your doctor decides based on your history and examination.

### The silent effect on the heart

High blood pressure makes the heart pump against extra force, day after day. Over years, the main pumping chamber thickens and becomes stiffer. Diabetes adds its own effects on the heart muscle and small blood vessels. Together, they are among the most common causes of heart failure.

The difficulty is that these changes are usually silent. A person can feel completely well, walk to the market and climb stairs, while the heart is already working under strain.

### Stage B: the stage before symptoms

Doctors describe heart failure in stages. Stage A means risk factors such as diabetes or high BP are present, but the heart is normal. Stage B means the heart has changed in structure or function, but there are no symptoms yet. Stage C is when symptoms appear.

Stage B is important because it is a window. Treatment at this stage aims to stop the heart from moving on to symptoms.

A 2025 study in the Indian Heart Journal, co-authored by Dr. Tamoghna Das, looked at 227 adults having routine echocardiography at a referral centre in North India. About half met the criteria for stage B heart failure, and reduced heart muscle strain was one of the most common findings. These results suggest that early heart changes may be more common than expected in everyday practice.

### What an echo shows

An echocardiogram uses sound waves to show:
- how strongly the heart pumps (ejection fraction)
- how well it relaxes and fills between beats
- thickness of the heart walls
- size of the heart chambers
- how the valves open and close
- pressure in the lungs

It takes about 20 to 40 minutes, needs no fasting and uses no radiation.

### What strain imaging adds

A standard echo can look normal while the heart muscle is already struggling. Strain imaging, also called speckle tracking, measures how well the muscle fibres shorten and stretch. Reduced strain can be an early sign of heart muscle stress, even when the ejection fraction is still normal.

> **What this means for you:** if strain imaging shows early changes, it does not mean you have heart failure with symptoms. It means your heart needs firmer protection: tighter BP control, good sugar control, cholesterol treatment, weight care and stopping tobacco.

### Who may benefit from an echo

Dr. Das may suggest an echo if you have:
- diabetes for several years, especially with high BP, kidney disease or high cholesterol
- high BP for a long time, or BP that is hard to control
- breathlessness, swelling of the feet, palpitations or reduced exercise ability
- an abnormal ECG or a heart murmur
- obesity with sleep apnea

The decision is made after a consultation, based on your own risks.

### FAQs

1. **I have diabetes but no heart symptoms. Do I need an echo?**
   Not everyone does. An echo is most useful when diabetes has been present for several years, especially with high BP, kidney disease, high cholesterol or obesity, or if you have any symptoms. Dr. Das decides after examining you and reviewing your risks.
2. **What does a normal ejection fraction mean?**
   It means the main pumping chamber is pushing out a normal share of blood with each beat, usually 55% or more. It does not rule out all heart problems. The heart can be stiff or under strain with a normal ejection fraction, which is why relaxation and strain are also checked.
3. **If my echo shows stage B changes, what happens next?**
   Treatment focuses on protecting the heart: firm BP control, sugar and cholesterol management, weight care, stopping tobacco and regular activity. Some medicines that protect the heart may be added. A repeat echo after some time shows whether the heart is stable or improving.
4. **How often should the echo be repeated?**
   It depends on the findings. A normal echo may not need repeating for some years unless symptoms appear. If changes are found, a repeat may be advised after 6 to 12 months. Dr. Das will tell you what suits your situation.

Sources: Gupta R, Das T, et al. Indian Heart Journal, 2025 · European Society of Cardiology, heart failure guidelines · American Society of Echocardiography guidance

**In brief**
- Snoring is common, but loud snoring with pauses in breathing or gasping can mean obstructive sleep apnea.
- Sleep apnea lowers oxygen many times a night and raises BP and heart risk.
- Daytime sleepiness, morning headaches and poor concentration are common signs.
- A sleep study confirms it.
- Treatment, including weight loss and CPAP, often improves sleep, energy and BP.

### Snoring or sleep apnea?

Snoring is the sound of air moving past relaxed tissues in the throat. Many people snore, especially after a tiring day, with a cold or after alcohol. On its own, it is often harmless.

Sleep apnea is different. The throat narrows so much that breathing stops or becomes very shallow for 10 seconds or more, many times every hour. Each time, oxygen drops and the brain wakes you briefly to restart breathing. You usually do not remember these awakenings, but your body feels them.

### Signs to look out for

At night (often noticed by a partner):
- loud snoring most nights
- pauses in breathing, then a gasp or snort
- choking or waking up breathless
- restless sleep, getting up often to pass urine

During the day:
- feeling sleepy while reading, watching TV or travelling
- dozing off at work or while driving
- morning headache or dry mouth
- poor memory and concentration
- irritability or low mood

### Why it matters for your heart and sugar

Every drop in oxygen sets off a stress response that raises heart rate and blood pressure. Over months and years, this contributes to:
- high BP, especially BP that stays high despite medicines
- irregular heart rhythm such as atrial fibrillation
- heart failure and stroke
- harder-to-control diabetes
- weight gain, which then worsens sleep apnea

> **Worth knowing:** Sleep apnea is one of the most common hidden causes of high BP that does not come down with medicines.

### Who is at higher risk

- Extra weight, especially around the neck and waist
- Collar size above 16 to 17 inches
- High BP, diabetes, heart rhythm problems
- Men, and women after menopause
- Older age
- Blocked nose, large tonsils, or a small lower jaw
- Alcohol or sleeping pills at night

### How it is tested

A sleep study records your breathing, oxygen level, pulse and position through the night. The key result is the number of breathing pauses or shallow breaths per hour. Dr. Das, who holds a Diploma in Obstructive Sleep Apnea, explains what the result means and the options.

### What treatment involves

- **Weight loss**, if needed, which often reduces apnea
- **Sleeping on your side** if apnea happens mainly on your back
- **Avoiding alcohol and sleeping pills** at night
- **Treating a blocked nose**
- **CPAP**, a small machine that blows gentle air through a mask to keep the throat open; the most effective treatment for moderate or severe sleep apnea
- **Dental devices or surgery** for selected people, through referral

### FAQs

1. **Can thin people have sleep apnea?**
   Yes. Weight is a major risk factor, but thin people can have sleep apnea because of a narrow airway, a small jaw, large tonsils or a blocked nose. If you have the signs, get checked whatever your weight.
2. **Is CPAP for life?**
   CPAP works while you use it, so most people with moderate or severe sleep apnea use it long term. If you lose a lot of weight or have other treatment, a repeat sleep study can show whether CPAP is still needed.
3. **Can snoring harm my partner?**
   It can disturb their sleep and cause tiredness. More importantly, your partner is often the first to notice breathing pauses. If they have noticed you stop breathing or gasp at night, please get checked.
4. **Will treating sleep apnea lower my BP?**
   In many people, yes, especially when BP has been hard to control. Treatment also improves energy, concentration and mood. BP medicines may still be needed, but often work better.
