**In brief**
- HbA1c shows your average blood sugar over roughly the last 3 months.
- Below 5.7% is normal, 5.7% to 6.4% is prediabetes, and 6.5% or more suggests diabetes.
- Many adults with diabetes aim for below 7%, but your own target may differ.
- You do not need to fast for an HbA1c test.
- Anaemia, some haemoglobin conditions and recent blood loss can make HbA1c misleading.

### What HbA1c measures

Sugar in the blood sticks to haemoglobin, the protein inside red blood cells. The higher your sugar has been, the more haemoglobin gets "sugar-coated". Red blood cells live for about 3 months, so the share of coated haemoglobin, shown as a percentage, reflects your average sugar over that time. Recent weeks count more than older ones.

That is why HbA1c is so useful. A single fasting sugar shows one moment. HbA1c shows the bigger picture.

### What the numbers mean

| HbA1c | What it suggests |
|---|---|
| Below 5.7% | Normal |
| 5.7% to 6.4% | Prediabetes: higher risk of diabetes; a good time to act |
| 6.5% or more | Diabetes, usually confirmed with a repeat test |

For people who already have diabetes, HbA1c shows how well the current plan is working.

### What should my target be?

For many adults with diabetes, the target is below 7%. Some people, such as younger adults early in diabetes, may aim lower if they can do it without low sugar. Others, such as older adults living alone, people with frequent lows or serious heart or kidney disease, may have a slightly higher target to stay safe.

Your target is a decision between you and your doctor. At Medcare, Dr. Das explains your number and your target at every review.

### How often should it be checked?

- Every 3 months when treatment is being changed or sugar is not on target
- Every 6 months when sugar is steady and on target
- Once a year as part of a check-up for adults at risk of diabetes

### When HbA1c can mislead

HbA1c depends on red blood cells, so anything that changes them can make the number look higher or lower than your real sugar:
- Iron deficiency anaemia can push HbA1c up
- Recent blood loss, blood transfusion or pregnancy can push it down
- Inherited haemoglobin conditions, such as haemoglobin E trait, common in Northeast India, can affect some test methods
- Advanced kidney disease can make results less reliable

If your HbA1c does not match your home readings, Dr. Das may suggest continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) to see what is really happening.

> **Tip:** you do not need to fast for HbA1c. At Medcare, HbA1c can be tested during your visit, with the result ready before you leave.

### Bringing HbA1c down

Small steady changes add up over 3 months:
- Smaller rice portions, with more vegetables and protein on the plate
- A 10 to 15 minute walk after main meals
- Fewer sweet drinks and sweets
- Regular sleep
- Taking medicines on time, every day
- Checking sugar at home as advised

### FAQs

1. **Do I need to fast for HbA1c?**
   No. HbA1c reflects your average sugar over about 3 months, so a recent meal does not change it. It can be checked at any time of day. If other fasting tests are planned on the same day, follow the fasting advice for those.
2. **Can HbA1c be normal even if my sugar is high after meals?**
   Yes, sometimes. HbA1c is an average, so high readings after meals can be balanced by lower readings at other times. Early in diabetes, after-meal sugar often rises first. CGM or after-meal checks help find this pattern.
3. **How quickly can HbA1c change?**
   HbA1c changes slowly, because it reflects about 3 months. You may see some change after 6 to 8 weeks, but the full effect of a new plan shows at about 3 months. That is why it is usually rechecked every 3 months.
4. **Is HbA1c alone enough to diagnose diabetes?**
   An HbA1c of 6.5% or more suggests diabetes, and it is usually confirmed with a repeat test or a sugar test. If you have clear symptoms and a high random sugar, one test may be enough. Your doctor will interpret it alongside your history.
