If your blood pressure is high at the clinic but normal at home, you may have white-coat hypertension. This happens when anxiety or alertness in a medical setting temporarily raises BP. It is common and usually confirmed with home BP monitoring or 24-hour ambulatory BP monitoring (ABPM). It still needs follow-up because it may carry some extra risk over time.

**Quick answer**
- White-coat hypertension means BP is high in the clinic but normal outside it.
- It is caused by a stress response in medical settings.
- Doctors confirm it with home readings or ABPM.
- The opposite, masked hypertension, is when clinic BP looks normal but home BP is high, which can be more concerning because it is easy to miss.

### Why Does BP Rise at the Clinic?

- Anxiety about the visit or test results
- Rushing to the appointment or climbing stairs
- Talking during the reading
- Not resting before measurement
- Wrong cuff size or poor arm position
- Caffeine or smoking before the visit

### What Is White-Coat Hypertension?

It is diagnosed when clinic BP is repeatedly high, but home or ambulatory readings are in the normal range. It affects many people and is more common in older adults and women.

### What Is Masked Hypertension?

This is the reverse situation. BP looks normal at the clinic but is high at home, at work or at night. It is more common in people with diabetes, kidney disease, smokers and those with stressful jobs. Because it is hidden, it can lead to untreated high BP and organ damage.

### How Do Doctors Confirm It?

#### Home BP monitoring

Taking readings twice daily for about a week using correct technique.

#### 24-hour ambulatory BP monitoring (ABPM)

A small device measures BP automatically every 15 to 30 minutes over a full day and night. It also shows night-time BP, which is an important risk marker.

### Is White-Coat Hypertension Harmless?

It is not always harmless. Some studies suggest people with white-coat hypertension may have a slightly higher long-term risk and may develop sustained high BP later. Doctors usually recommend lifestyle changes and regular monitoring rather than immediate medicines, unless other risk factors are present.

### What Can You Do?

- Arrive early and rest before the reading
- Avoid caffeine and smoking before the visit
- Sit quietly with back supported and feet flat
- Ask for repeat readings after a few minutes
- Keep a home BP log to share
- Follow healthy habits: less salt, regular activity, healthy weight

### Key Takeaways

- White-coat hypertension is high BP only at the clinic.
- Home monitoring or ABPM confirms it.
- Masked hypertension is high BP outside the clinic.
- White-coat hypertension still needs follow-up.
- Correct technique avoids false high readings.

### Final Thoughts

Your BP reading at one visit does not tell the whole story. Home and ambulatory readings give a clearer picture and help avoid both over- and under-treatment. ### FAQs

1. **What is white-coat hypertension?**
   It is when blood pressure is high in a clinic or hospital but normal at home or during daily life. It happens because of a stress response to the medical setting.
2. **Do I need medicine for white-coat hypertension?**
   Often not right away. Doctors usually recommend lifestyle changes and regular monitoring. Medicines may be considered if you have other risks like diabetes, kidney disease or signs of heart strain.
3. **What is ABPM?**
   Ambulatory BP monitoring uses a small device worn for 24 hours to measure BP at regular intervals, including during sleep. It gives a complete picture of your BP pattern.
4. **What is masked hypertension?**
   It is when BP is normal at the clinic but high at home, work or night. It can be missed without home or ambulatory monitoring and may need treatment.
5. **Can anxiety cause high BP readings?**
   Yes. Anxiety, rushing and talking can temporarily raise BP. Resting for five minutes and staying silent during the reading help get a more accurate result.
6. **Should I buy a BP monitor if I have white-coat hypertension?**
   Yes. A validated home BP monitor helps track your true BP and supports better decisions with your doctor. Use correct technique and keep a log.
