**In brief**
- Obesity is a medical condition that can affect blood sugar, blood pressure, cholesterol, sleep and cardiovascular health.
- BMI is useful for an initial assessment, but waist circumference and metabolic health can provide important additional information.
- Losing even a moderate amount of weight can improve blood sugar, BP and other metabolic risk factors.
- Effective weight management usually involves food habits, physical activity, sleep and long-term follow-up.
- Prescription medicines, including GLP-1 and dual GIP/GLP-1 medicines, may be appropriate for some people.
- When obesity occurs with diabetes, hypertension, high cholesterol or sleep apnea, these problems are best considered together.

Obesity is more than a high number on the weighing scale. Excess body fat can affect the way the body handles insulin, blood pressure, cholesterol and sleep. Over time, these changes can increase the risk of diabetes and heart disease.

Treatment therefore needs to look beyond weight loss alone. The aim is to improve health, reduce future risk and help you maintain those changes over the long term.

### What is obesity?

Obesity is a chronic medical condition in which excess body fat can affect health.

The first measurement doctors commonly use is body mass index, or BMI. It is calculated from your height and weight and gives an initial indication of whether your weight may be contributing to health risks.

BMI has limitations. It does not distinguish between muscle and fat, and it does not tell us where body fat is stored.

That is why a proper assessment may also include waist circumference, body composition, blood pressure, blood sugar, cholesterol, sleep and other medical conditions.

This broader assessment is particularly useful in people who have diabetes, prediabetes, high BP or other metabolic problems.

### Is BMI enough to diagnose obesity?

Not by itself.

BMI is a useful screening measure, but it does not provide a complete picture of metabolic health. Two people with the same BMI may have very different amounts of body fat, muscle and abdominal fat.

Waist circumference is useful because excess abdominal fat is associated with insulin resistance and cardiovascular risk.

A doctor may therefore look at several measurements together.

| **Assessment** | **Why it matters** |
|---|---|
| BMI | Gives an initial weight category |
| Waist circumference | Indicates abdominal fat |
| Body composition | Gives an estimate of fat and lean tissue |
| Blood pressure | Identifies hypertension and cardiovascular risk |
| HbA1c or blood glucose | Checks for prediabetes or diabetes |
| Lipid profile | Assesses LDL, HDL and triglycerides |
| Sleep assessment | Looks for possible sleep apnea |

For Indian and South Asian patients, metabolic problems can sometimes occur at lower BMI levels than expected. This is one reason waist measurement and blood tests should not be ignored simply because BMI does not appear very high.

### Why does obesity increase the risk of diabetes?

The connection between obesity and Type 2 diabetes is closely related to insulin resistance.

Insulin is a hormone that helps glucose move from the bloodstream into the body's cells. When the body becomes less responsive to insulin, the pancreas has to produce more insulin to keep blood glucose under control.

For some people, this compensation becomes less effective over time. Blood glucose then starts to rise.

This can first appear as prediabetes and may eventually progress to Type 2 diabetes.

The good news is that insulin resistance can improve with appropriate weight loss, regular physical activity and better food habits.

If you have already been diagnosed with diabetes, weight management can become an important part of your overall diabetes treatment. You can read more about [diabetes care at Medcare](/diabetes-care/).

#### Can losing weight improve Type 2 diabetes?

Yes. Weight loss can improve blood glucose control in many people with Type 2 diabetes.

You do not necessarily need to reach an "ideal" weight before seeing health benefits. A moderate reduction in body weight may improve insulin sensitivity and other metabolic factors.

The right target is different for each person. Your doctor may consider your current weight, diabetes control, medicines, BP, cholesterol, activity level and other health conditions when setting a goal.

Some people with Type 2 diabetes may eventually achieve remission after substantial and sustained weight loss. Remission means blood glucose remains below the diabetes range without the usual glucose-lowering treatment for a period of time. It should not be interpreted as a permanent cure, and continued monitoring remains important.

### How does obesity affect blood pressure?

Obesity can contribute to high blood pressure through several changes in the body, including effects on the kidneys, blood vessels, hormones and the nervous system.

The heart may also need to work harder when a person carries excess body weight.

One difficulty with hypertension is that you may not feel anything at all. Someone can have both obesity and high BP and still feel completely well.

This is why BP should be measured rather than judged by symptoms.

If your readings are repeatedly high, discuss them with a doctor. You can also read the [high blood pressure guide at Medcare](/heart-health/high-blood-pressure/) for more information about diagnosis and monitoring.

### Does obesity increase heart disease risk?

It can.

Obesity often occurs alongside other cardiovascular risk factors such as high BP, diabetes, abnormal cholesterol and physical inactivity. These factors can interact rather than acting independently.

Over time, high blood sugar, high BP and abnormal blood lipids can contribute to damage within the arteries. This can increase the risk of coronary artery disease, heart attack and other cardiovascular problems.

This does not mean that every person with obesity will develop heart disease.

Your actual risk depends on the complete picture, including age, blood pressure, diabetes, cholesterol, smoking, family history and other factors.

### How does obesity affect cholesterol?

Obesity can be associated with an unfavourable lipid profile.

Some people may have higher triglycerides and lower HDL cholesterol. LDL cholesterol may also be elevated, although the pattern differs between individuals.

These changes matter because abnormal cholesterol contributes to atherosclerosis, the gradual build-up of fatty deposits in artery walls.

If you have obesity and an abnormal lipid profile, simply focusing on weight may not be enough. Cholesterol and other cardiovascular risk factors may also need treatment.

### Can obesity cause sleep apnea?

Obesity is one of the important risk factors for obstructive sleep apnea.

During sleep, the muscles around the upper airway relax. In some people, the airway becomes repeatedly narrowed or blocked. Breathing may stop briefly before restarting, sometimes with loud snoring, gasping or choking.

Common symptoms include:

- Loud or persistent snoring
- Pauses in breathing noticed by a partner
- Gasping or choking during sleep
- Excessive daytime sleepiness
- Morning headaches
- Poor concentration
- Feeling tired despite apparently getting enough sleep

Sleep apnea can also occur with high BP, insulin resistance and Type 2 diabetes.

If you have these symptoms, particularly loud snoring combined with daytime sleepiness, it is worth discussing them with a doctor.

### What other health problems can be associated with obesity?

The effects of obesity are not limited to diabetes and heart disease.

Depending on the individual, excess weight may be associated with:

- Prediabetes and Type 2 diabetes
- High blood pressure
- Abnormal cholesterol and triglycerides
- Sleep apnea
- Fatty liver
- Joint pain and reduced mobility
- Some hormonal and reproductive problems

Not every health problem in a person with obesity is necessarily caused by obesity. A proper medical assessment is needed to understand what is contributing to the symptoms or abnormal test results.

### What does medical weight loss actually mean?

Medical weight loss is not simply being told to "eat less and exercise more".

It starts with understanding why the person has gained weight, what health problems are already present and what treatment is realistic.

Depending on the situation, a medical weight-management plan may include:

- Weight, waist and body-composition assessment
- Review of eating patterns
- Physical activity assessment
- Sleep assessment
- Review of medicines that may affect weight
- Screening for diabetes and other metabolic conditions
- Nutrition and behaviour changes
- Prescription weight-loss medicines when appropriate
- Regular follow-up

Not everyone needs medication.

Similarly, there is no single diet that works for every person. A useful plan needs to fit the person's health, food preferences, work routine, family situation and ability to maintain the changes.

### What should you eat if you are trying to lose weight?

A weight-loss diet does not have to look like a Western diet.

If rice is part of your normal meals, you do not necessarily need to stop eating rice. The portion, what you eat with it and the rest of the day's food matter.

A practical Indian meal may include a sensible portion of rice or roti, vegetables and a suitable source of protein such as dal, pulses, fish or eggs.

Some everyday changes can make a meaningful difference:

- Reduce oversized portions.
- Include vegetables regularly.
- Include an appropriate protein source in meals.
- Cut down on sugary drinks and frequent sweetened tea.
- Keep fried snacks and sweets for occasional eating.
- Be careful with portions during festivals and family gatherings.
- Avoid crash diets that are difficult to continue.

"Sugar-free" does not automatically mean calorie-free or suitable in unlimited quantities.

The right food plan also depends on whether you have diabetes, kidney disease, high cholesterol or another medical condition.

### Do you need to stop eating rice to lose weight?

No.

Rice can be part of a weight-management diet. The more useful question is how much rice you are eating, what accompanies it and how the overall day's diet looks.

For someone with diabetes, large portions of rice may cause a significant rise in blood glucose. Adjusting the portion and combining it with vegetables and protein may be more practical than removing rice completely.

The right portion varies from person to person.

### How much exercise is needed for weight management?

Regular physical activity helps with weight control, fitness, blood glucose and cardiovascular health.

Walking is often a good starting point. It can be fitted into daily life without needing a gym.

Depending on your age, fitness and medical condition, activity may include:

- Brisk walking
- Cycling
- Swimming
- Gardening
- Household work
- Field work
- Strength exercises

If you have chest discomfort, significant breathlessness, uncontrolled BP or a known heart condition, discuss exercise intensity with your doctor before starting strenuous activity.

> **Tip:**A simple activity routine that you follow consistently is more useful than an ambitious exercise plan that lasts only a few weeks.

### When are weight-loss medicines considered?

Lifestyle changes remain an important part of treatment, but some people need more than lifestyle changes alone.

Prescription weight-loss medicines may be considered when obesity or overweight is affecting health and lifestyle measures have not produced enough improvement.

A doctor may consider your:

- BMI and waist circumference
- Blood sugar
- BP
- Cholesterol
- Sleep apnea
- Other medical conditions
- Current medicines
- Previous weight-management attempts
- Possible benefits and side effects

The decision is individual.

A medicine that is appropriate for one patient may not be suitable for another.

### What are GLP-1 medicines?

GLP-1 medicines act on hormone pathways involved in appetite, food intake and blood glucose regulation.

They can increase fullness and reduce appetite. Some also slow the movement of food through the stomach.

Different GLP-1 medicines have different approved uses. Some are used primarily in diabetes, while others are approved for chronic weight management in selected patients.

Gastrointestinal side effects are common and may include nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea and constipation.

These medicines also have important precautions and are not appropriate for everyone.

Medical supervision is particularly important because the doctor needs to consider your existing illnesses, other medicines and the reason for treatment.

Medcare provides information about [GLP-1 weight-loss medicines](/weight-management/glp-1-medicines/) as part of its weight-management care.

### What are dual GIP/GLP-1 medicines?

Some newer medicines act on two hormone pathways, GIP and GLP-1.

They affect appetite and glucose regulation and may produce substantial weight loss in appropriate patients.

Choosing between medicines is not simply a matter of choosing the one with the highest reported weight-loss effect. The decision also depends on diabetes status, other health conditions, side effects, contraindications, treatment goals and whether long-term treatment is appropriate.

Weight regain can occur after these medicines are stopped. That is one reason weight management needs to be approached as a long-term health issue.

### Are weight-loss injections suitable for everyone?

No.

The suitability of a weight-loss medicine depends on the specific drug and the person's medical history.

Pregnancy, plans for pregnancy, certain gastrointestinal conditions, other medical problems and existing medicines can affect the decision. Some medicines also have specific contraindications.

Do not use another person's injection or purchase prescription weight-loss medicines without proper medical advice.

The medicine, dose and duration of treatment should be decided by a doctor.

### How much weight loss is needed to improve heart health?

There is no single number that applies to every patient.

Even a moderate reduction in body weight may improve BP, blood glucose, triglycerides and other metabolic measures.

But heart health is not determined by weight alone.

A person who loses weight but continues to have uncontrolled BP, high LDL cholesterol or diabetes may still have significant cardiovascular risk.

For this reason, weight management should sit alongside appropriate treatment of other risk factors.

### What happens if you have obesity and diabetes together?

This combination is common and needs careful management.

Weight loss may improve insulin sensitivity and glucose control. It may also help with BP, triglycerides, physical mobility and sleep.

If your blood sugar improves, your diabetes medicines may sometimes need adjustment. That decision must come from your doctor.

Do not stop insulin or diabetes tablets on your own.

If you monitor your blood sugar at home or use continuous glucose monitoring, those readings can be useful during follow-up because they show how your glucose responds to food, activity and treatment.

### Why do people regain weight after losing it?

Weight regain is common and does not mean a person lacks discipline.

After weight loss, the body can respond with increased hunger and changes in energy expenditure. These biological responses can make maintaining a lower weight difficult.

This is one reason short-term diets often fail to produce lasting results.

A long-term plan may include regular activity, practical food habits, adequate sleep, behaviour changes and medical treatment when appropriate.

For some patients, prescription treatment may need to continue for longer periods.

### What should you do if you have obesity, diabetes and high BP?

It is useful to look at these conditions together.

A medical review may include your:

- Weight and waist measurement
- BP readings
- HbA1c and blood glucose
- Cholesterol and triglycerides
- Diabetes medicines
- Sleep symptoms
- Activity level
- Eating habits
- Cardiovascular risk

The treatment plan can then focus on the problems that are most relevant to you.

For example, someone with obesity, Type 2 diabetes and high BP may need a different approach from someone with obesity but normal blood sugar and BP.

> **Tip:**Take your recent reports and current medicine list to your appointment. Looking at several results together often gives a clearer picture than looking at one number alone.

### What can you do at home to manage your weight?

Start small.

If you normally eat rice twice a day, you do not have to completely remove it overnight. Look at the portion and the rest of the meal first.

Choose vegetables more often. Include a suitable protein source. Reduce sugary drinks and frequent sweetened tea. Keep sweets and fried snacks for occasional eating.

Try to move regularly through the day. Walking is a simple option for many people.

Sleep also deserves attention. Poor sleep can make appetite and weight management more difficult. If you snore loudly, wake up gasping or feel very sleepy during the day, ask your doctor whether sleep apnea should be assessed.

### What are common myths about obesity?

#### "Obesity is simply a lack of willpower."

No. Eating habits are only one part of the picture. Genetics, sleep, physical activity, medicines, medical conditions, environment and appetite regulation can all influence body weight.

#### "A very strict diet is the fastest way to solve obesity."

Not necessarily. A restrictive diet may produce quick weight loss, but maintaining it can be difficult. A realistic plan that fits everyday life usually has a better chance of lasting.

#### "Weight-loss injections are the easiest solution."

No. These are prescription medicines, not shortcuts. They have benefits, side effects and contraindications and should be used when medically appropriate.

#### "Once I lose weight, I can stop my diabetes or BP medicines."

No. Weight loss can improve glucose and BP, but medicines should only be changed after reviewing your readings and overall health.

#### "If my BMI is normal, I cannot have metabolic problems."

Not necessarily. Waist circumference, blood sugar, cholesterol, BP and family history can also affect metabolic risk.

### When should you see a doctor about your weight?

Consider a medical assessment if:

- Your weight has been increasing steadily.
- Your waist size has increased.
- You have prediabetes or diabetes.
- Your BP readings are repeatedly high.
- Your cholesterol or triglycerides are abnormal.
- You snore loudly or feel unusually sleepy during the day.
- Your weight is affecting your mobility.
- You repeatedly lose and regain weight.
- You are considering weight-loss medicines.
- You gained weight after starting a medicine.

A medical assessment can help determine whether you need lifestyle support, further testing, prescription treatment or a combination of these.

### When is emergency care needed?

Obesity itself is not usually an emergency. The symptoms associated with heart attack or stroke are.

Seek urgent medical help if you develop:

- Severe or persistent chest pain or pressure
- Severe breathlessness
- Fainting or sudden collapse
- Sudden weakness on one side of the body
- Sudden difficulty speaking
- Heavy sweating together with chest discomfort

If you think you may be having a heart attack, stroke or another medical emergency, call **108 or 112**.

### Key takeaways

- Obesity can affect blood sugar, BP, cholesterol, sleep and cardiovascular health.
- BMI is useful, but it should be considered alongside waist circumference and metabolic health.
- Weight management is usually a long-term process rather than a short diet programme.
- Food habits, physical activity and sleep remain important even when medication is prescribed.

### FAQs

1. **Can obesity cause Type 2 diabetes?**
   Yes. Excess body fat can increase insulin resistance, which makes it harder for the body to control blood glucose. Over time, this can contribute to prediabetes and Type 2 diabetes in people who are susceptible. Weight loss and regular physical activity can improve insulin sensitivity and may make diabetes easier to manage.
2. **How much weight should I lose to improve my health?**
   There is no single target for everyone. A moderate reduction in weight can improve blood sugar, BP and other metabolic measures in many people. Your target should take into account your starting weight, diabetes status, BP, cholesterol, physical activity and other health conditions.
3. **Are GLP-1 medicines safe for weight loss?**
   They can be appropriate for selected patients, but they are not suitable for everyone. Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea and constipation are common possible side effects. The doctor also needs to consider your medical history, other medicines and possible contraindications before prescribing one.
4. **Can I eat rice while trying to lose weight?**
   Yes. Rice does not have to be completely removed from a weight-management diet. Portion size and the rest of the meal matter. A reasonable portion of rice with vegetables and a suitable protein source can fit into a balanced Indian diet. People with diabetes may need individual advice about the amount.
5. **Can losing weight reduce heart disease risk?**
   It can improve several factors associated with cardiovascular disease, including BP, blood glucose and triglycerides. However, weight loss does not remove every cardiovascular risk. Diabetes, high BP and abnormal cholesterol may still require treatment even after you lose weight.
6. **Will I regain weight after stopping weight-loss medicine?**
   Weight regain can happen after stopping treatment. Changes in appetite and energy use can make maintaining weight difficult. This does not mean the treatment was unsuccessful. Long-term food habits, physical activity, sleep and, for some people, continued medical treatment can all have a role in maintaining the result.
