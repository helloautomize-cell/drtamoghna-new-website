A good LDL cholesterol level depends on your overall risk of heart disease. For a healthy adult with low risk, a lower LDL is generally better, and many labs show under 100 mg/dL as optimal. For people with diabetes, high blood pressure or other risk factors, doctors often aim lower. For those who already have heart disease or very high risk, targets are lower still.

**Quick answer**
- There is no single LDL number that suits everyone.
- Doctors set your LDL target based on age, diabetes, BP, smoking, kidney health, family history and existing heart disease.
- People at higher risk need lower LDL targets.
- The lab's reference range is a general guide, not your personal goal.

### What Is LDL?

LDL (low-density lipoprotein) carries cholesterol through the blood. When levels are high, LDL can build up in artery walls, forming plaques that narrow arteries and raise the risk of heart attack and stroke. This is why it is called "bad" cholesterol.

### How Are LDL Targets Decided?

Doctors look at your overall cardiovascular risk:

#### Lower risk

Healthy adults without major risk factors. Lab ranges serve as a general guide, and lifestyle is the main focus.

#### Moderate to high risk

People with diabetes, high BP, smoking, obesity, kidney disease or a strong family history. Doctors usually aim for lower LDL levels and may recommend statins.

#### Very high risk

People who have had a heart attack, stroke, stent or bypass, or those with diabetes plus other major risks. Targets are the lowest, and combination treatment may be needed.

Indian and international guidelines provide specific numbers for each group. Your doctor will explain your personal target.

### Why Are Indians at Higher Risk?

South Asians tend to develop heart disease at younger ages and often have a pattern of high triglycerides, low HDL and abdominal fat. This makes early LDL control especially important.

### How Can You Lower LDL?

#### Lifestyle

- Reduce saturated fats, such as ghee, butter, full-fat dairy and red meat
- Avoid trans fats from vanaspati, fried snacks and bakery items
- Eat more fibre from oats, pulses, vegetables and fruits
- Include nuts and seeds in moderation
- Exercise regularly
- Maintain a healthy weight
- Stop smoking

#### Medicines

Statins are the main medicines for lowering LDL and reducing heart risk. Other medicines may be added if targets are not reached.

### How Often Should LDL Be Checked?

Usually once a year for most adults with risk factors, and 6 to 12 weeks after starting or changing medicines.

### Key Takeaways

- A good LDL depends on your heart risk.
- Higher risk means a lower LDL target.
- Diabetes usually calls for lower LDL goals.
- Diet, exercise and statins all help.
- Ask your doctor for your personal target.

### Final Thoughts

Your LDL number is one of the most powerful tools for preventing heart disease. Knowing your target helps you and your doctor make the right choices. ### FAQs

1. **Is an LDL of 130 mg/dL normal?**
   For a low-risk adult, it may be within the lab's borderline range. For someone with diabetes or heart disease, it is usually too high. Your doctor will interpret it based on your overall risk.
2. **Can LDL be too low?**
   Very low LDL levels achieved with treatment are generally considered safe in studies. Your body makes the cholesterol it needs. Your doctor will monitor you while on treatment.
3. **Why does my doctor want my LDL lower than the lab range?**
   Lab ranges are general. If you have diabetes, high BP or heart disease, a lower LDL further reduces your risk of heart attack and stroke. Targets are personalised.
4. **How quickly can statins lower LDL?**
   Statins usually lower LDL noticeably within 4 to 6 weeks. Your doctor will check your lipid profile after starting treatment to see if the target is reached.
5. **Can diet alone bring LDL to target?**
   Diet and exercise can lower LDL, especially for mild elevations. People at higher risk often also need medicines to reach their targets.
6. **Does thin body size mean LDL is fine?**
   No. Slim people can have high LDL due to genetics or diet. Everyone over a certain age, especially with a family history, should have their lipid profile checked.
