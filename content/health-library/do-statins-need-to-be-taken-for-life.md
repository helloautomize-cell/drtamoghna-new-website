For many people, statins are a long-term treatment because the risk they protect against, heart attack and stroke, continues over time. Cholesterol often rises again when statins are stopped. However, the need for statins depends on your overall risk, and some people may have their dose or treatment adjusted with lifestyle changes. Any change should be made only with your doctor's advice.

**Quick answer**
- Statins usually work only while you take them.
- Stopping them can cause LDL to rise back within weeks and may increase heart risk, especially in people with diabetes or existing heart disease.
- Some people with lower risk who make major lifestyle changes may be able to adjust treatment, but this should be decided with a doctor.

### Why are statins often long-Term?

#### Ongoing risk

Diabetes, high BP, family history and age do not disappear, so the need for protection continues.

#### LDL returns when stopped

Statins reduce the liver's cholesterol production. When stopped, LDL usually rises again.

#### Plaque stability

Statins help stabilise plaques in arteries, making them less likely to rupture. Stopping may remove this benefit.

### Who usually needs them long-Term?

- People who have had a heart attack, stroke, stent or bypass
- Many people with diabetes, especially over 40 or with other risks
- People with very high LDL from genetic causes
- Those with high overall cardiovascular risk

### Can statins ever be reduced or stopped?

In some situations, doctors may consider adjusting treatment:

- Lower-risk individuals who make major lifestyle improvements
- Older adults with limited life expectancy or many medicines
- People with significant side effects, where alternatives are discussed

This decision should always be made with your doctor.

### Common concerns about statins

#### Muscle aches

Some people notice muscle aches. Many cases are mild and may not be caused by the statin. Your doctor can check, adjust the dose or switch medicines.

#### Liver effects

Mild changes in liver tests can occur but serious liver harm is rare. Tests may be done as needed.

#### Blood sugar

Statins may slightly raise blood sugar in some people. For most, heart protection benefits outweigh this small risk.

#### "Statins are harmful long-term"

Statins have been studied for decades in large numbers of people. For those at risk, the benefits usually outweigh the risks.

### Tips for taking statins

- Take them at the same time each day
- Do not stop suddenly without advice
- Report severe muscle pain, weakness or dark urine
- Continue healthy diet and exercise, which add benefit
- Check lipid levels as advised

### Key takeaways

- Statins usually work only while taken.
- Many people need them long-term.
- Stopping can increase heart risk.
- Side effects are usually mild and manageable.
- Never stop statins without medical advice.

### Final thoughts

Statins are one of the most well-studied ways to protect the heart. Understanding why they are prescribed helps you stay on track with confidence.


### FAQs

1. **What happens if I stop taking statins?**
   LDL cholesterol usually rises back to previous levels within weeks. In people with heart disease or high risk, stopping may increase the chance of heart attack or stroke. Talk to your doctor first.
2. **Can I stop statins if my cholesterol is normal now?**
   A normal level on statins often means the medicine is working. Stopping usually brings levels back up. Your doctor will decide based on your overall risk, not only your current number.
3. **Do statins damage the liver?**
   Serious liver damage from statins is rare. Mild changes in liver tests can occur. Your doctor may check liver function if needed and adjust treatment if there are concerns.
4. **Are muscle pains common with statins?**
   Some people experience muscle aches, but many cases are not caused by the statin. If you have muscle pain, tell your doctor. They may adjust the dose or try a different statin.
5. **Can lifestyle changes replace statins?**
   For low-risk people, lifestyle changes may be enough. For people with diabetes or heart disease, lifestyle changes usually work alongside statins rather than replacing them.
6. **Do statins raise blood sugar?**
   Statins may slightly raise blood sugar in some people. For most people with diabetes or high heart risk, the heart protection benefits outweigh this small effect.
