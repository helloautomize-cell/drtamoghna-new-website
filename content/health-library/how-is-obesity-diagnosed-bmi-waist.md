Obesity is diagnosed using body mass index (BMI) as a starting point, along with waist circumference and an assessment of metabolic health, such as blood sugar, blood pressure and cholesterol. Asian Indians tend to carry more fat around the abdomen and develop diabetes and heart disease at lower body weights, so lower BMI and waist cut-offs are often used in India.

**Quick answer**
- Doctors usually calculate BMI from height and weight, measure waist circumference to check abdominal fat and look for health effects like high sugar, BP, cholesterol, fatty liver or sleep apnea.
- For Indian adults, overweight and obesity are often defined at lower BMI levels than in Western countries.
- Some newer approaches focus on whether excess fat is already affecting health.

### What Is BMI?

BMI = weight (kg) divided by height (m) squared.

Commonly used Asian Indian cut-offs:

- **Normal:** 18.5 to 22.9
- **Overweight:** 23 to 24.9
- **Obesity:** 25 and above

WHO global cut-offs are higher, with obesity at 30 and above. Your doctor will explain which ranges they use.

### Why Is BMI Not Enough?

BMI does not show where fat is stored or how much is muscle. A muscular person may have a high BMI with little fat, while a person with normal BMI may carry harmful belly fat.

### Waist Circumference

Waist size reflects abdominal fat, which is closely linked to diabetes and heart disease. Commonly used Indian cut-offs for increased risk are:

- Men: 90 cm or more
- Women: 80 cm or more

Measure at the level of the navel, standing relaxed, after breathing out.

### Metabolic Health Checks

- Fasting blood sugar and HbA1c
- Blood pressure
- Lipid profile
- Liver tests and ultrasound for fatty liver
- Kidney function
- Thyroid tests if needed
- Sleep apnea screening if snoring or daytime sleepiness are present

### Other Assessments

- Body composition analysis
- Waist-to-height ratio (aiming for less than half)
- Joint problems, mobility and breathlessness
- Emotional health and eating patterns

### Why Does Diagnosis Matter?

Obesity is a chronic medical condition, not a lack of willpower. Proper diagnosis helps guide treatment, which may include lifestyle changes, structured programmes, medicines or, for some people, surgery.

### Key Takeaways

- BMI is a starting point, not the full picture.
- Indian adults often use lower BMI cut-offs.
- Waist size shows harmful abdominal fat.
- Metabolic tests show the health impact.
- Obesity is a chronic medical condition.

### FAQs

1. **What BMI is considered obese for Indians?**
   Many Indian guidelines consider a BMI of 25 or more as obesity and 23 to 24.9 as overweight. These cut-offs are lower than WHO global values because of higher metabolic risk at lower weights.
2. **Why is waist size important?**
   Waist size reflects abdominal fat, which is strongly linked to diabetes, high BP and heart disease. Even with a normal BMI, a large waist may increase health risk.
3. **How do I measure my waist correctly?**
   Stand relaxed, breathe out gently and measure around your abdomen at the level of the navel. Keep the tape snug but not tight, and parallel to the floor.
4. **Can I be healthy and overweight?**
   Some people with higher weight have normal metabolic tests for a time, but risk often increases over the years. Regular check-ups help monitor sugar, BP and cholesterol.
5. **Is obesity a disease?**
   Many medical organisations now consider obesity a chronic disease that affects many body systems. It is influenced by genetics, environment, hormones and behaviour, not just willpower.
6. **What tests should I do if I am overweight?**
   Common tests include blood sugar, HbA1c, lipid profile, liver tests, kidney function and BP. Thyroid tests and sleep apnea screening may be added based on symptoms.
